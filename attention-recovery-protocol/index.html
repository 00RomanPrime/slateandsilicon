The 30-Day Attention
Recovery Protocol.

Thirty days. Five phases. No willpower required.

You didn't lose your attention. It got diluted — pulled in thirty directions a day by systems built to keep it moving. That's not a discipline problem. It's a trained response to an environment engineered for interruption.

The guilt that shows up after an hour disappears into a feed. The irritation when a task takes longer because your attention keeps sliding sideways. None of that is a personality problem showing up late in life. It's what the system was built to produce.

Field Note — Roman Prime, Operator 00: I built the first version of CORE for myself, after I noticed I'd open my phone mid-sentence and not remember deciding to. Thirty days didn't fix that. It showed me exactly when it happens, every time — and once I could see it, I could actually catch it before it ran.

Seven days breaks a habit. It doesn't rebuild the structure underneath it. The reflexive open, the check before you've finished a thought — that wasn't built in a week, and it doesn't come apart in one either.

A 2026 longitudinal study tracking 277 people over seven months found that overload correlated less with raw time spent online and more with how fragmented that time was — frequent, short, isolated re-entries scattered across the day. Duration measures exposure. It doesn't measure the pattern actually causing the strain.

Thirty days gives the pattern time to show itself, and gives you time to build something in its place. Five phases, in order: baseline, then friction, then boundaries, then Runway, then seven days with the feed closed — then reintegration. Nothing here is instant. Nothing here should be.
